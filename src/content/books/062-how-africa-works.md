Great book! Once again I feel fascinated by Joe Studwell's interpretation of developmental forces on a continental scale. When I read his previous book, How Asia Works, I was rather unprepared for the amount of economic and financial terminology he used. Now, after reading a lot of books on similar topics since then it feels like a real coming home to some extent that I felt WAY more in control of the material. It also pushed me to shore up my African geography and learn all of the continent's capitals!

This book was split into 3 main sections: (a) how we got to where we are now on the scale of the entire continent and what are the things that typify most, if not all African nations; (b) deep dives into 4 countries that show promise for the future as of now (Botswana, Mauritius, Ethiopia, & Rwanda); and (c) where do we go from here and what are the things Studwell believes are the most important for development for lower-income countries, agricultural policy and manufacturing policy. 

I felt the first section, although had some super interesting context that I'll mention below, was a little all over the place. I understand his motivation to paint with a giant brush the entire continent by using examples, but in a continent of 55 countries it felt like we were pinging back and forth across a huge area with lots of details that was nigh impossible to retain. Luckily I had reviewed my geography otherwise I would have been lost unless I referred to the tiny country map at the start of the book. 

With that critique out of the way, one of the most interesting pieces to me that explains a lot of Africa's previous and current woes is Studwell's claim that the main attribute that has held the region back is its lack of population density. In many other nations most people were land poor and labor was cheap. In Africa, there has always been plenty of land, but not nearly enough people. So historically invaders aren't taking land, they are taking the scarce thing. People. This exacerbates the problem of there being further not enough people to work the land. This fact has also meant that in the modern day even though the continent is poor, its labor isn't necessarily as cheap as it should be. Another downstream consequence is that people cannot benefit from the benefits that city living offers, people specializing, infrastructure becoming cost effective to build, etc. 

One of the other claims that Studwell makes that lends itself to the population density argument is that the continent has more than its fair share of the global disease burden. 

The two things that Studwell thinks Africa needs more of, and the nations that have succeeded have had some of these things, are smallholder agriculture, and government led and nurtured domestic industrial policy. This was his thesis in How Asia Works about what separates China, South Korea, Japan, and Taiwan from the likes of Indonesia, the Philippines, Malaysia, etc. He makes the argument, in a convincing way, that Africa would indeed see growth, even with all of its ills, from the same model that caused some of East Asia's most miraculous growth. 

There is so much more to say, but I'm going to leave it there. I felt I learned a ton and would definitely buy the book and read it again at some point.
