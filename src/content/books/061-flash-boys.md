I very much enjoyed this one. I wasn't sure I would like Michael Lewis' style/books. I expected the book to be somewhat in line with 1929, which I had read right before this, in that it would tell a good story, but I wouldn't learn much about the finance. I was wrong! I feel like I understood the finance of high-frequency trading MUCH better now. I felt that the narrative was used as a vehicle to teach how the finance worked and why high frequency trading is parasitic. I also felt that I was kind of the perfect target audience, as someone with solid technical knowledge and solid financial knowledge, especially in a scene where a bunch of finance coders were talking about one of their own who had been sentenced to jail/prison. 

The book helped me understand why "speed matters" when it comes to finance, what dark pools are, what front-running is, and helped me think of the different players (and exchanges) differently. It also showed me that I was almost a naive perpetrator in that system because I believe virtu financial is a parasitic institution that takes money from investors that are trying to properly allocate capital and shielding what they do in a veil of "adding liquidity" which is almost definitely not true in the spirit of the definition. 

The main ways, as I understand it, that high frequency traders make money is:
- exploiting discrepancies between exchanges: if a price changes in one exchange, than a HFT will buy/sell on the other markets before they update their prices
- frontrunning traders: They will put the minimum buy/sell order for every stock in an exchange (the first one a trader will hit before their signal goes to the other exchanges). When this small trade is initiated the HFT determines if this is the first part of a larger order, and if so goes to the other exchanges before the trader can and buys/sells as much as they can and then offers the other side of that trader at the higher/lower price

They end each day even, without taking any positions, so they take almost no risk and don't obviously provide benefit to the market. Now, Virtu refers to itself as a market maker, which it likely does, and is a service. But it can also exploit its position in the market and the information it gets by the nature of their role. 

Overall, I was pleasantly surprised! I really liked it and will try out his other finance books, starting with Liar's Poker.
